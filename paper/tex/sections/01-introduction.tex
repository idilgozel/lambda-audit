\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a recurrent agent outperforms a memoryless one on a partially observed
task, the result is read as evidence about \emph{memory}: the task needed it
and the architecture supplied it. Most of what is known about memory in deep
reinforcement learning rests on comparisons of this shape. Both agents in such
a comparison are usually trained with an actor--critic method whose advantage
estimator, GAE($\lambda$) \citep{schulman2016gae}, bootstraps the critic over
an effective horizon of $(1-\gamma\lambda)^{-1}$ steps. Under partial
observability the critic is a function of the observation alone, so it is
biased wherever the observation aliases states with different futures --- and
the horizon sets how much of that bias the policy gradient inherits. The
horizon is set by $\lambda$, a hyperparameter chosen by convention (0.95 in
most codebases, 1.0 in the POPGym reference results \citep{morad2023popgym})
and rarely varied in memory studies. The quantity it interacts with is the
correlation time of the hidden state, which is exactly what a memory benchmark
manipulates.

This paper makes that interaction exact. In a family of partially observed
linear-Gaussian control problems with quadratic cost, the fixed point of the
averaged actor--critic update under an observation-restricted TD($\lambda$)
critic is computable in closed form (\S\ref{sec:theory}). On the reference
instance it moves from a gain of $24.79$ at $\lambda=0$ to $1.51$ at
$\lambda=1$, against a true memoryless optimum of $1.649$: the learning target
of the ``memoryless baseline'' is displaced by an order of magnitude under a
hyperparameter that is rarely reported to two decimals, and the displacement
is away from the optimum. The excess cost this induces collapses at a
computable threshold $\lambda_{1/2}$, and that threshold is the paper's central
empirical result: frozen before the runs, it is hit within $0.01$ on all four
instances tested, with absolute errors of $0.00021$, $0.00014$, $0.00101$ and
$0.00497$ (\S\ref{sec:results}).

Two further results say when this matters and when it does not. A
\emph{scope proposition} (\S\ref{sec:theory}) shows that when actions do not
influence the hidden state the critic's bias cancels identically from every
action preference at every $\lambda$; the effect requires a hidden coordinate
the action can \emph{steer}. Both sides of that boundary are confirmed on
external benchmarks under one harness: on position-only CartPole, where the
condition holds, all three registered predictions pass (memoryless
performance steps down below the horizon by $-0.021$, 95\% CI
$[-0.029,-0.014]$, with no step above it); on two POPGym games where the
latent is fixed at reset and actions only reveal it, the predicted effect is
absent, with the measured memory advantage at $\lambda=0.95$ \emph{smaller}
than at $\lambda=1.0$. And the threshold is not an artifact of the minimal
instance: the same structure appears at state dimensions 2--4 for arbitrary
Prony memory kernels, and it survives a cubic damping the theory cannot
absorb --- at a perturbation that moves the floor cost by $2.5\times$, the
linearized theory still locates $\lambda_{1/2}$ within $0.0017$
(\S\ref{sec:results}). Thresholds and cost levels are robust across
dimension, nonlinearity and drive distribution; equilibrium \emph{locations}
are not, and that split predicts which of the paper's own predictions passed.

A second threshold is derived from the same machinery: an off-manifold
stability index whose destabilising term carries the bootstrap weight
$(1-\lambda)/\lambda$ itself, predicting that below a critical $\lambda^\ast$
training does not converge and its measured quality depends on when it is
stopped. Its experimental record is mixed --- the onset ordering and the
unstable side of a perturbation-decay test pass, the location and the stable
side miss their registered thresholds --- and a rule fixed before the data
existed demotes it to a suggestive calculation. We report it as such.

\paragraph{Method.} Registered predictions are recorded in executable
preregistration files, with deployment checks for unset constants and explicit
checks for existing data. These checks do not retrospectively authenticate
freeze dates; limits of the saved chronology and post-data scorer amendments
are disclosed in \S\ref{sec:discussion}.
Of twenty-five scored predictions, eleven passed, fourteen failed, and
every one is reported (Table~\ref{tab:ledger}). Most of the failures are the
scope proposition doing its job on the wrong side of its boundary, or a
location prediction the theory itself marks as fragile; three are genuine
misses of the mechanism claim, handled by the rule above. Capability gates are
reported separately in both directions, because a failed gate means a
prediction was not scored, not that it was refuted. The preregistration is
how the theory was tested, not what the paper claims; the audit's history,
including the two experiments whose design errors we own, is in the appendix.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{The confound, made exact.} A closed-form aliased equilibrium
  map $k_{\mathrm{AC}}(\lambda)$, its cost law, and a threshold
  $\lambda_{1/2}$ predicted prospectively within $0.01$ at four instances.
\item \textbf{A scope law with evidence on both sides.} A proposition stating
  where the effect is absent, confirmed in-domain by a decoupled control arm
  and externally by one benchmark where it appears and two where it does not.
\item \textbf{Generality of the thresholds.} Kernel- and dimension-general
  formulas; robustness to a 50\% nonlinear perturbation; and a robust/fragile
  split --- cost and thresholds versus equilibrium location --- that
  organises the results.
\item \textbf{The audit as an artifact.} Executable preregistrations with
  deployment checks, disclosed amendments, a released diagnostic, and a
  ledger of every registered outcome, released with the testbed and harness.
\end{enumerate}
